\section*{Abstract}
\addcontentsline{toc}{section}{Abstract}

Tunnelling magnetoresistance (TMR) sensors offer chip-scale, high-sensitivity magnetic field detection that is attractive for CubeSat attitude determination and control, yet the technology has little space heritage, and little device-level data exists on how a commercial part behaves when it is saturated by a strong field or after irradiation. This report characterises a low-cost commercial TMR sensor, the CT100, using two custom printed circuit boards: a standalone board exposing the raw bridge output, and an integrated board that adds an STM32 microcontroller and an independent current-sense reference to drive and log a controlled test field from a PCB trace. The saturation ceiling was within about 5\% of $V_{\text{DD}}/3$ on every unit and symmetric about zero, and the output recovered from saturation in 40 to \SI{85}{\milli\second} as an exponential decay with a time constant of about 9 to \SI{14}{\milli\second}. At a \SI{10}{\hertz} magnetometer rate this requires the first one to two samples after a saturating event to be discarded. On a fresh sensor, saturation shifted the zero-field output by about \SI{16}{\milli\volt} (roughly \SI{1}{\milli\tesla}) while the gradient changed by only 0.5\% and the response stayed linear, so a one-point re-zeroing is needed after saturation. The measured current-to-output gradient was 77 to 87\% of the theoretical value, consistent with datasheet tolerance and field-model uncertainty. After a total ionising dose of \SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}(Si), no change attributable to radiation was found in the ceiling, recovery or gradient, and ferrite shielding produced no measurable change. The results suggest that the CT100 is a promising low-cost COTS candidate for CubeSat magnetometry, although its resolution at geomagnetic field levels remains to be demonstrated.

\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:intro}

CubeSats are nanosatellites (1--10~kg) built from standardised 10~cm cubic units, offering low-cost, rapid access to space relative to traditional monolithic satellite missions. This miniaturisation is achieved at the expense of severe Size, Weight, Power, and Cost (SWaP-C) constraints, which particularly affect the Attitude Determination and Control System (ADCS): the subsystem responsible for orienting a CubeSat for communications, power generation, and payload pointing. Onboard magnetometers are the primary means by which a CubeSat senses its attitude relative to the Earth's magnetic field, so the choice of magnetometer technology is central to ADCS performance within a tightly constrained mass, volume, and power budget.

Heritage fluxgate magnetometers offer excellent sensitivity but are too large and power-hungry for CubeSat platforms, motivating a shift toward chip-scale magnetoresistive sensors. Among these, Tunnelling Magnetoresistance (TMR) sensors offer the highest sensitivity of the commercially available options, in a footprint of only a few millimetres square. However, TMR remains comparatively immature for space use -- typically cited at Technology Readiness Level (TRL) 3 -- with comparatively little published characterisation data under space-relevant stresses such as strong transient fields from onboard actuators (e.g.\ magnetorquers) and ionising radiation.

This report documents the design, construction, and testing of a project evaluating a commercial-off-the-shelf (COTS) CT100 TMR sensor for CubeSat ADCS/EPS applications, carried out by the author. Two custom PCBs were developed: a primary board exposing the raw CT100 bridge output, and an integrated telemetry board combining the sensor with microcontroller-based logging and an independent current-based field reference. The work addresses four objectives:

\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item characterise the CT100 sensor itself -- its operating principle, its Wheatstone-bridge topology, and its saturation and hysteresis behaviour;
\item design and build hardware capable of generating a known, controllable test field and logging the sensor's response to it automatically;
\item quantify the sensor's sensitivity, saturation-recovery time, and the accuracy of the field model used to drive it; and
\item assess the sensor's tolerance to Total Ionising Dose (TID) radiation representative of the CubeSat orbital environment, with and without magnetic shielding.
\end{enumerate}

A parallel thermal-cycling test campaign investigating TMR temperature sensitivity and thermal-shock response was led by project partner Daniel Tieu and is reported separately; it is outside the scope of this report.

The remainder of this report follows the project's own design and testing sequence. Section~\ref{sec:litreview} reviews the relevant literature. Section~\ref{sec:research-question} sets out the resulting research question, aims, and objectives. Section~\ref{sec:ct100} characterises the CT100 sensor itself. Section~\ref{sec:instrumentation} introduces the instrumentation used throughout. Section~\ref{sec:standalone-board} covers the standalone sensor board and sensor-only saturation testing. Section~\ref{sec:integrated-board} covers the design of the integrated board and its field-excitation method, including validation of the field model against simulation. Section~\ref{sec:instr-validation} cross-validates the instrumentation used throughout. Section~\ref{sec:nonirr-characterisation} presents the non-irradiated sensitivity and saturation dataset. Section~\ref{sec:radiation-testing} covers radiation testing, and Section~\ref{sec:shielding} covers magnetic shielding, including the combined radiation-plus-shielding result. Sections~\ref{sec:discussion} and~\ref{sec:conclusions} discuss the findings and conclude.

\section{Literature Review}
\label{sec:litreview}

\subsection{Magnetometer Technologies for CubeSat ADCS}
\label{sec:magnetometer-tech}

Fluxgate magnetometers (FGMs) have been used in space exploration for decades: an alternating current drives a coil-wrapped permeable core toward saturation, and an external field produces a measurable imbalance between drive and sense coil currents. FGMs offer excellent sensitivity (of order \SI{10}{\pico\tesla\per\sqrt{\hertz}}) but are far too large and power-hungry for CubeSat platforms, typically weighing over \SI{1}{\kilo\gram} and drawing more than \SI{10}{\watt} \cite{survey-magnetic-sensing}. Magneto-inductive (MI) sensors, which sense an external field through the change it induces in the resonant frequency of an LR oscillator, trade sensitivity for a much smaller footprint and lower power draw \cite{survey-magnetic-sensing}.

Hall-effect sensors are inexpensive and, because they contain no ferromagnetic material, immune to magnetic saturation. However, their comparatively low sensitivity confines them to detecting fields above roughly \SI{1}{\milli\tesla}, and while flux concentrators can improve their effective sensitivity, doing so reintroduces the mass and volume penalty that SWaP-C constraints are meant to avoid \cite{amr-gmr-hall-bridging}. Magnetoresistive sensors -- AMR, GMR, and TMR -- instead occupy the sub-milliTesla sensitivity range needed for geomagnetic-field sensing while remaining chip-scale. AMR sensors (typically permalloy thin films) achieve high resolution and bandwidth but saturate above roughly \SI{1}{\milli\tesla} and often require periodic magnetic reset \cite{amr-gmr-hall-bridging}. GMR sensors, based on a multilayer stack of alternating ferromagnetic and non-magnetic conductive layers, offer higher sensitivity again but are more prone to hysteresis-induced offset and can be permanently degraded by strong fields \cite{amr-gmr-hall-bridging}. Chip-scale magnetoresistive sensing has already reached flight heritage: the AMR-based MAGIC instrument, for example, has demonstrated a \SI{104}{\gram}, \SI{0.5}{\watt} dual-sensor vector magnetometer in orbit on the RadCube CubeSat \cite{magic-radcube}.

TMR sensors extend this trend further. They are built from magnetic tunnel junctions, whose spin-dependent tunnelling through an ultra-thin barrier was first demonstrated by Julli\`ere \cite{julliere1975} and made practical at room temperature with crystalline MgO barriers \cite{parkin2004,yuasa2004}. TMR is the newest of the magnetoresistive technologies and is generally cited at TRL 3, compared with TRL 9 for AMR and GMR \cite{alves-thesis}. The specific device used in this project is described in Section~\ref{sec:ct100}.

\subsection{Radiation Tolerance of TMR Sensors}
\label{sec:rad-tolerance-lit}

Because TMR is comparatively new to the space sector, published radiation-tolerance data is limited, though what exists is promising. Alves' assessment of magnetoresistive sensors for space applications supports the conclusion that TMR devices are radiation-hard \cite{alves-thesis}. Published testing of TMR magnetic sensors based on MTJ structures found their performance unchanged under bias voltages of $+5$, $0$, and $-5\,\text{V}$ after a Total Ionising Dose (TID) of \SI{5}{\mega rad}(Si) using $\gamma$-ray and X-ray sources \cite{tmr-bias-radiation}, and MTJs intended for a CubeSat magnetometer have been tested to \SI{100}{\kilo rad} \cite{nguyen2010}. These doses are well above the \SI{100}{\kilo rad}(Si) level typically used to qualify a device as radiation-hardened. The result is physically consistent with the crystalline MgO barrier at the heart of the MTJ: ionising radiation predominantly damages devices through electron-hole pairs that become trapped at defect sites, permanently shifting threshold voltages, a mechanism that primarily affects the \emph{amorphous} SiO\textsubscript{2} gate oxide used in conventional CMOS devices \cite{tid-basics-lanl}, and for which MgO's ordered crystal lattice offers far fewer trap sites.

Commercial magnetometers are also routinely radiation-tested before use in space. Regoli et al.\ irradiated nine PNI RM3100 magnetometers, a COTS magneto-inductive sensor, to \SI{300}{\kilo rad}(Si) and found that most units tolerated the full dose \cite{regoli2020}, and a commercial CubeSat magnetometer is specified at a component-level TID of \SI{10}{\kilo rad} \cite{nss_magnetometer}. Testing several units of a low-cost COTS part is therefore an accepted approach. The tolerance of TMR junctions, however, has mostly been shown for the sensing element itself rather than for a packaged COTS part working in a circuit. This literature evidence motivated the TID testing described in Section~\ref{sec:radiation-testing}.

\subsection{Strong Magnetic Fields on CubeSats and Offset Shift in TMR Sensors}
\label{sec:strong-field-lit}

CubeSat magnetometers sit close to magnetic sources, chiefly the magnetorquers used for attitude control, and are therefore usually mounted as far from the spacecraft as possible, ideally on a boom \cite{nss_magnetometer}. A typical CubeSat magnetorquer board has a nominal magnetic moment of about $\SI{0.2}{\ampere\metre\squared}$ and can sit inside the electronics stack \cite{isis_imtq}. The on-axis field of a current loop of radius $R$ carrying current $I$ is $B = \mu_0 I R^2 / \left(2(r^2+R^2)^{3/2}\right)$ \cite{openstax_up2}. Far from the loop ($r \gg R$) this becomes $B = \mu_0 m / (2\pi r^3)$, where $m = I\pi R^2$ is the magnetic moment. Applied to the magnetorquer, this gives $\SI{5}{\milli\tesla}$ at $\SI{2}{\centi\metre}$, $\SI{1.5}{\milli\tesla}$ at $\SI{3}{\centi\metre}$ and $\SI{0.3}{\milli\tesla}$ at $\SI{5}{\centi\metre}$. These are roughly 5 to 100 times Earth's field of 25 to $\SI{65}{\micro\tesla}$ \cite{wmm}, but they are within the CT100's $\pm\SI{50}{\milli\tesla}$ operating range \cite{ct100_datasheet}. (The point-dipole estimate overstates the field at distances comparable to the coil size, so these values are an order-of-magnitude guide.) A correctly operating magnetorquer would therefore not normally saturate a TMR sensor, although it would swamp the geomagnetic signal unless the readings are timed around it. Saturation needs a much stronger or closer source, such as a permanent magnet near the sensor, a magnetorquer within about a centimetre, or magnetising equipment during ground handling and test. It is best treated as an off-nominal event, and the questions that matter are whether the sensor survives it and what it leaves behind.

The manufacturer states that its XtremeSense TMR sensors survive any magnetic field amplitude, but notes that after a strong field the offset can shift, that this shift is stable and irreversible, and that, because such events are rare, an offset recalibration is typically recommended \cite{allegro_an133}. This confirms that the effect is expected, but it gives no magnitude for a specific part and does not consider a platform on which the sensor's signal is only a few tens of microtesla, where an offset of even one millitesla would dominate.

\subsection{Research Gap}
\label{sec:gap}

Taken together, the literature establishes four things. TMR is among the most sensitive chip-scale magnetoresistive technologies suitable for CubeSat-class ADCS/EPS use (Section~\ref{sec:magnetometer-tech}). TMR junctions have been shown to tolerate radiation at the element level, and COTS magnetometers are routinely radiation-tested (Section~\ref{sec:rad-tolerance-lit}). Strong fields are an off-nominal hazard for a CubeSat magnetometer, and the manufacturer acknowledges a lasting offset shift after one (Section~\ref{sec:strong-field-lit}). Yet, in the sources found for this review, no study reports device-level data for a specific low-cost packaged COTS TMR sensor working inside a real circuit that covers (a) how quickly it recovers from saturation, in terms an on-board computer designer can use, (b) how large the lasting offset shift is and whether its gradient and linearity survive, and (c) its behaviour after irradiation, using a test method that can be reproduced with ordinary bench equipment. To the best of the author's knowledge no published study reports these for the CT100. This gap motivates the research question and objectives in Section~\ref{sec:research-question}.

\section{Research Question, Aims, and Objectives}
\label{sec:research-question}

\textbf{Research question.} Are low-cost, commercial-off-the-shelf TMR sensor operationally viable for CubeSat ADCS/EPS use, specifically with respect to (a) its behaviour when driven into saturation by an unexpectedly strong magnetic field and then recovered, including any lasting change to its output, and (b) any change in its sensing performance following Total Ionising Dose radiation exposure representative of the CubeSat orbital environment?

This question follows directly from the gap identified in Section~\ref{sec:gap}: the literature supports TMR's theoretical suitability and likely radiation tolerance, and the manufacturer acknowledges an offset shift after strong fields, but it does not provide device-level evidence for a specific, inexpensive COTS part of the kind a resource-constrained CubeSat programme would actually select.

\textbf{Aims.} This project aims to generate that device-level evidence for the CT100, and, in doing so, to build and validate a low-cost, automatable test methodology that could be reused for other candidate COTS TMR devices without requiring specialised space-qualification equipment.

\textbf{Objectives.} The research question and aims above translate into the following concrete objectives, which structure the remainder of this report:
\begin{enumerate}[label=(\roman*)]
\item characterise the TMR sensor itself -- its operating principle and its saturation ceiling explored in Section~\ref{sec:ct100};
\item design and build hardware capable of generating a known, controllable test field and logging the sensor's response to it automatically, without relying on specialised or costly space-test equipment, this can be found in Sections~\ref{sec:instrumentation}--\ref{sec:integrated-board};
\item quantify the sensor's sensitivity and saturation-recovery time, and the accuracy of the field model used to drive it, establishing a non-irradiated baseline cross-validated across instruments evaluated in Sections~\ref{sec:nonirr-characterisation} and~\ref{sec:instr-validation};
\item measure, with a controlled before-and-after test on a fresh sensor, how saturation changes the sensor's output offset, gradient and linearity (Section~\ref{sec:hysteresis});
\item evaluate whether low-cost ferrite shielding materially changes the measured response (Section~\ref{sec:shielding}); and
\item assess the sensor's tolerance to Total Ionising Dose radiation representative of the CubeSat orbital environment, with and without magnetic shielding, against the same validated methodology (Section~\ref{sec:radiation-testing}).
\end{enumerate}

\subsection{Hypotheses}
\label{sec:hypotheses}

The objectives above are tested through six hypotheses. Each one is stated with the criterion used to judge it, so that the results can support or reject it, and each is linked to the section where it is tested. 

\begin{enumerate}[label=\textbf{H\arabic*:}, leftmargin=*]
\item \textbf{Saturation ceiling.} The output saturates at $V_{\text{out}} = \pm V_{\text{DD}}/3$, as derived from the bridge topology in Section~\ref{sec:bridge-topology}, and the positive and negative ceilings are equal in magnitude. It is supported if every measured ceiling lies within a few percent of $V_{\text{DD}}/3$, allowing for the tolerance of the element resistances.

\item \textbf{Field model and sensitivity.} The gradient of $V_{\text{out}}$ against trace current matches the gradient predicted from the field model and the datasheet sensitivity. It is supported if the measured gradient corresponds to a sensitivity inside the datasheet range of 3.8 to $\SI{5.2}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}$ (explored in Section~\ref{sec:sensitivity-fit}).

\item \textbf{Saturation recovery.} After saturation the output returns to its baseline within one sample interval of a typical CubeSat magnetometer read at \SI{10}{\hertz} ($\SI{100}{\milli\second}$), so no samples need to be discarded. It is supported if the worst-case recovery time, including a safety margin, is shorter than this interval (Sections~\ref{sec:standalone-sat-recovery} and~\ref{sec:sat-recovery-integrated}).

\item \textbf{No lasting effect of saturation.} Driving the sensor into saturation leaves no lasting change. It is supported if, on a sensor with no magnetic history, the zero-field baseline after saturation is within one ADC step of the baseline before saturation and the gradient is unchanged within session-to-session variation (Section~\ref{sec:hysteresis}).

\item \textbf{Radiation tolerance.} A total ionising dose of $\SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}$(Si) causes no measurable permanent change, as the literature on MgO-barrier MTJs predicts (Section~\ref{sec:rad-tolerance-lit}). It is supported if the saturation ceiling, recovery time and gradient of irradiated sensors differ from non-irradiated sensors by less than the variation between non-irradiated units and sessions (Section~\ref{sec:radiation-testing}).

\item \textbf{Shielding.} Enclosing the sensor in ferrite shielding measurably changes its response by reducing the influence of the ambient field. It is supported if the shielded response differs from the unshielded response by more than the session-to-session variation (Section~\ref{sec:shielding}).
\end{enumerate}

\section{The CT100 Sensor}
\label{sec:ct100}

\subsection{Device Overview}
\label{sec:ct100-overview}

The \mbox{CT100LW-FS6} Tunnel Magnetoresistance (TMR) sensor was selected primarily due to its thermal stability, differential output, small footprint, and cost. The device is rated for an operating temperature range of \SI{-40}{\degreeCelsius} to \SI{150}{\degreeCelsius} \cite{ct100_datasheet}, making it highly suitable for  thermal cycling  tests relevant to my partner Daniels Tieu's work. Also, its differential output architecture provided high Common-Mode Rejection Ratio (CMRR), mitigating common-mode noise across its magnetic dynamic range of $\pm50~\text{mT}$ with a low linearity error of $\pm0.5\%$ (between $-20~\text{mT}$ and $+20~\text{mT}$) \cite{ct100_datasheet}. 

Integrated TMR sensor ICs were explored but ultimately were decided against as raw signal behavior of the sensor was desired,  discrete analog sensor enables direct characterization and isolated testing of the TMR elements itself and provided additional flexibility for system integration if needed. Furthermore, its SOT-23-6 package minimizes both PCB footprint and mass, aligning with  Size, Weight, and Power (SWaP) constraints typical of CubeSat applications. Sourced at a unit cost of \$0.92 via DigiKey (as of April 2026) \cite{digikey_ct100}, it provided a highly economical solution for our testing.

The \mbox{CT100LW-FS6} supports a supply voltage range of \SI{1.0}{\volt} to \SI{5.5}{\volt} \cite{ct100_datasheet}. Because the sensor's differential output sensitivity scales proportionally with supply voltage ($S \propto V_{\text{DD}}$), operating at a higher $V_{\text{DD}}$ increases the full-scale differential output voltage swing per unit magnetic field ($\text{mV/T}$). This larger voltage swing enhances the signal-to-noise ratio (SNR). However, a supply voltage of $V_{\text{DD}} = \SI{3.30}{\volt}$ was selected to match the \SI{3.30}{\volt} operating voltage of a STM32 microcontroller. This eliminates the need for additional voltage regulators or signal conditioning level-shifters, simplifying the power electronics if system integration was required.

\subsection{Physical Operating Principle}
\label{sec:ct100-principle}

The CT100LW-FS6, like other TMR devices is built around magnetic tunnel junctions (MTJs): a pinned ferromagnetic reference layer, an ultra-thin (\textasciitilde\SI{2}{\nano\metre}) insulating crystalline magnesium oxide (MgO) layer and a magnetically free ferromagnetic layer \cite{parkin2004,yuasa2004}. 

Conduction through the junction is done under the quantum tunnelling property, in which electrons have a nonzero probability of passing through the MgO barrier. Figure~\ref{fig:mtj_equivalent_structure} denotes the MTJs equivalent circuit model.

The basic operation of the MTJ comes down to how easily electrons can pass through the MgO barrier placed between two ferromagnetic layers. The bottom layer serves as a fixed reference with a locked magnetic direction, while the top free layer can rotate its magnetic orientation $360^\circ$ to align an external magnetic field.

When an external field aligns the free layer's direction  ($0^\circ$) with the bottom reference layer, electrons hop across the insulating barrier much more easily. Higher electron movement per unit of time represents a greater amount of electrical charge $\Delta Q$ passing through the barrier over time $\Delta t$. By definition, current is the rate of charge flow per unit of time:
\begin{equation}
    I = \frac{\Delta Q}{\Delta t}
\end{equation}
Therefore, this increased charge movement results in a higher current flow $I$ for a constant supply voltage $V_{\text{DD}}$, putting the sensor into its parallel, lowest resistance state $R = \frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{I}$. Conversely, when the field lines points in the opposite direction ($180^\circ$), fewer electrons can cross, restricting current flow and increasing resistance. If the external magnetic field is applied orthogonal to the fixed reference direction, the sensor cannot detect it, making the element effectively blind to perpendicular fields. Hence, a TMR element can be modelled as a variable resister where it's resistance varies dependant on the polarity and strength of an external magnetic field.

% 2. MATHEMATICAL DERIVATION SECOND
To predict the maximum differential output voltage prior to saturation, the CT100's internal TMR Wheatstone bridge (Figure~\ref{fig:wheatstone_comparison}a) was evaluated theoretically. An equivalent schematic with explicitly labeled internal elements is provided in Figure~\ref{fig:wheatstone_comparison}b to aid in following the derivation below.  According to the device datasheet \cite{ct100_datasheet}, the per-element resistance ranges from $R_{\min} \approx \SI{20}{\kilo\ohm}$ where the element experiences parallel magnetic alignment, lowest-resistance state) to $R_{\max} \approx \SI{40}{\kilo\ohm}$ (antiparallel magnetic alignment, highest-resistance state). 

As a result of this Wheatstone-bridge configuration, the largest change in $\left|V_{\text{out}} \right|$ is $\frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{3}$:
\begin{equation}
   \left| \Delta V_{\text{out,max}} \right| = \frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{3}
\end{equation}
This maximum output swing is bounded by each of the internal TMR element resistance range ($R_{\min} \approx \SI{20}{\kilo\ohm}$ to $R_{\max} \approx \SI{40}{\kilo\ohm}$).

The voltage potentials at the two central output nodes, $X_1$ and $X_2$, as seen in Figure~\ref{fig:wheatstone_comparison}b are determined by the independent voltage divider ratios along the bridge:
\begin{equation}
    X_1 = V_{\text{DD}} \cdot \left( \frac{R_3}{R_1 + R_3} \right), \qquad X_2 = V_{\text{DD}} \cdot \left( \frac{R_4}{R_2 + R_4} \right)
    \label{eq:node_voltages}
\end{equation}

The general differential output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ across nodes $X_1$ and $X_2$ is expressed as:
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{out}} = X_1 - X_2 = V_{\text{DD}} \left( \frac{R_3}{R_1 + R_3} - \frac{R_4}{R_2 + R_4} \right)
    \label{eq:vout_general}
\end{equation}

At $B = \SI{0}{\tesla}$, all four TMR elements exhibit nominal, equal resistance ($R_1 = R_2 = R_3 = R_4$), yielding balanced potentials $X_1 = X_2 = \frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{2}$ and $V_{\text{out}} = \SI{0}{\volt}$. 

A key operational characteristic of the CT100 is that an external magnetic field applied along its sensitive axis drives opposing element pairs in opposite resistance directions. As one pair (\(R_1, R_4\)) decreases toward \(R_{\min}\), the complementary pair (\(R_2, R_3\)) increases toward \(R_{\max}\) or vice versa. This response is governed by the orientation of the pinned ferromagnetic reference layer within each TMR element: \(R_1\) and \(R_4\) share an identical reference layer alignment, whereas \(R_2\) and \(R_3\) are pinned in the opposite direction.

 At full magnetic saturation, the opposing resistor pairs settle at either $R_{\min}$ and $R_{\max}$. For this derivation, an external magnetic field polarity is assumed such that:

\begin{equation}
R_1 = R_4 = R_{\min}, \quad \text{and} \quad R_2 = R_3 = R_{\max}
\label{eq:tmr-saturation-resistances}
\end{equation}

Substituting these saturation values into Equation~\ref{eq:node_voltages} gives the node potentials at full saturation:
\begin{equation}
    X_{1,\text{sat}} = V_{\text{DD}} \cdot \left( \frac{R_{\max}}{R_{\min} + R_{\max}} \right), \qquad X_{2,\text{sat}} = V_{\text{DD}} \cdot \left( \frac{R_{\min}}{R_{\max} + R_{\min}} \right)
\end{equation}

Subtracting $X_{2,\text{sat}}$ from $X_{1,\text{sat}}$ yields the maximum theoretical differential output voltage $V_{\text{out,sat}}$:
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{out,sat}} = X_{1,\text{sat}} - X_{2,\text{sat}} = V_{\text{DD}} \left( \frac{R_{\max} - R_{\min}}{R_{\max} + R_{\min}} \right)
    \label{eq:vout_sat_derived}
\end{equation}

Substituting the CT100 datasheet resistance values \cite{ct100_datasheet} ($R_{\min} = \SI{20}{\kilo\ohm}$ and $R_{\max} = \SI{40}{\kilo\ohm}$):
\begin{equation}
    V_{\text{out,sat}} = V_{\text{DD}} \left( \frac{\SI{40}{\kilo\ohm} - \SI{20}{\kilo\ohm}}{\SI{40}{\kilo\ohm} + \SI{20}{\kilo\ohm}} \right) = V_{\text{DD}} \left( \frac{20}{60} \right) = \frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{3}
\end{equation}

This  derivation proves that the maximum differential output voltage is  bound to  $\left| \Delta V_{\text{out,max}} \right| = \frac{V_{\text{DD}}}{3}$. For a supply voltage of $V_{\text{DD}} = \SI{3.30}{\volt}$, the theoretical saturation output  $\left|V_{\text{out,sat}} \right|$ is $ \SI{1.10}{\volt}$. This is experimentally validated in Section~\ref{sec:standalone-sat-recovery}.

The direction of the applied field along the sensitive axis decides which resistor pair moves to $R_{\min}$, so the bridge can saturate at either polarity. The two limits, $V_{\text{out}} = +V_{\text{DD}}/3$ and $V_{\text{out}} = -V_{\text{DD}}/3$, are called the \textbf{positive ceiling} and the \textbf{negative ceiling} respectively. The negative ceiling follows from the derivation above by swapping $R_{\min}$ and $R_{\max}$. In this report, \emph{ceiling} means the saturation limit of $V_{\text{out}}$, and its sign states which of the two was reached.

\subsection{Saturation Testing Methodology}


\label{sec:sat-test-methodology}


For this board, magnetic saturation was induced using an external LM-1618 Neodymium magnet. This manual method of bringing the magnet toward and away from the sensor was the simplest available stimulus for an initial characterization, though it is not inherently repeatable or automatable.

To reliably drive the device into saturation and characterize the sensor's transient recovery and hysteresis under worst case situations, the device was intentionally driven far beyond its standard operating limits. The CT100 sensor has an operating magnetic field range of $\pm 50$\,mT. However, its absolute maximum threshold where device reliability may be affected is specified at $\pm 200$\,mT \cite{ct100_datasheet}.

Saturation was induced with an LM-1618 neodymium disc magnet, \SI{25}{\milli\metre} in diameter and \SI{5}{\milli\metre} thick, with a remanence $B_r$ of 1180 to \SI{1220}{\milli\tesla} \cite{lm1618_datasheet}. The remanence is a property of the material, not the field outside the magnet. On the axis of a disc magnet of radius $R$ and thickness $L$, the field at distance $z$ from its face is
\begin{equation}
B(z) = \frac{B_r}{2}\left[\frac{L+z}{\sqrt{R^2+(L+z)^2}} - \frac{z}{\sqrt{R^2+z^2}}\right],
\label{eq:disc-magnet}
\end{equation}
which gives about \SI{223}{\milli\tesla} at the face, \SI{152}{\milli\tesla} at \SI{5}{\milli\metre} and \SI{86}{\milli\tesla} at \SI{10}{\milli\metre}. Within about a centimetre the field is therefore well beyond the CT100's $\pm\SI{50}{\milli\tesla}$ operating range, which guarantees saturation, while staying close to or below its \SI{200}{\milli\tesla} absolute-maximum rating \cite{ct100_datasheet}, since the package itself keeps the sensing element away from the magnet face.

During testing, the differential output voltage was monitored using an oscilloscope to confirm that it hit the saturation limit of \(\left| \Delta V_{\text{out,max}} \right| \approx V_{\text{CC}} / 3\) which we derived in Section~\ref{sec:bridge-topology}. Once the output voltage was confirmed to be at this saturation ceiling, the magnet was rapidly removed. The oscilloscope then captured the transient recovery, recording the time required for the output to settle back to a steady-state baseline under no external magnetic field excitation.

Every saturation result in this report is presented in the same table format, with the same definitions:
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Sample:} the unit tested. Samples 1 to 4 are sensor-only boards (1 and 2 non-irradiated, 3 and 4 irradiated). Boards A, B and C are integrated boards (A non-irradiated, B irradiated, C a fresh sensor used for the hysteresis test in Section~\ref{sec:hysteresis}).
\item \textbf{Ceiling (V):} the output voltage $V_{\text{out}}$ reached at saturation. Its sign shows which ceiling was reached, so each sample has one row per ceiling.
\item \textbf{Baseline (mV):} the output once it has settled after the field is removed.
\item \textbf{Swing (V):} the size of the drop from the ceiling to the baseline, $|{\rm ceiling} - {\rm baseline}|$.
\item \textbf{Recovery (ms):} the time from the start of the fall until the output has settled at its baseline. On the oscilloscope this is read with cursors; for the MCU (SWV) traces it is the time until the output stays within $\SI{5}{\milli\volt}$ of its final baseline. Five millivolts is also about the oscilloscope's vertical resolution at $\SI{500}{\milli\volt}$/div, so the two definitions of ``settled'' are comparable.
\end{itemize}

In small satellite architectures, secondary magnetometers used for coarse attitude determination or payload monitoring are typically polled by the OBC  rates between \SI{1.0}{\hertz} and \SI{10.0}{\hertz}. For a magnetometer with an update rate of $f_s$, consecutive readings are spaced $T_s = 1/f_s$ apart. At a $\SI{10}{\hertz}$ update rate specified for a representative CubeSat magnetometer \cite{mag_sample}, $T_s = \SI{100}{\milli\second}$. Comparing this directly against the measured recovery time (Table~\ref{tab:sat-ceiling-all}) gives a margin of only $\SI{26.5}{\milli\second}$ ($26\%$ of $T_s$) using the mean recovery time ($\SI{73.5}{\milli\second}$), narrowing to $\SI{15}{\milli\second}$ ($15\%$) against the single worst trial observed ($\SI{85}{\milli\second}$).

\begin{equation}
    f_{\text{rec,min}} = \frac{1}{t_{\text{rec,max}}} = \frac{1}{\SI{76.5}{\milli\second}} \approx \SI{13.07}\hertz > \SI{10.0}\hertz
\end{equation}
 
This margin is thinner than it first appears, and a simple comparison of recovery \emph{frequency} against sampling \emph{frequency} is not sufficient to assess it. A magnetorquer firing may not be synchronised to the magnetometer's sampling clock, so it may occur at any point within a sampling interval. In the worst-case alignment, a magnetorquer deactivates immediately prior to a scheduled ADC sample tick. Under this condition, the sensor is sampled while under saturation recovary regardless of whether $f_{\text{rec,min}} > f_s$. Processing an unrecovered transient reading introduces an artificial impulse into onboard state estimators (e.g., Kalman Filters), which can trigger over corrections in attitude or control loop instability. 

Therefore, the critical design metric is establishing a time-domain guard window that guarantees complete baseline restoration with margin. Applying a standard $1.5\times$ to $2.0\times$ engineering safety factor to the worst-case observed recovery duration ($t_{\text{rec,max}} = \SI{85.0}{\milli\second}$) defines a recommended guard window of $\SI{127.5}{\milli\second}$ to $\SI{170.0}{\milli\second}$:

\begin{equation}
    T_{\text{guard}} = (1.5 \text{ to } 2.0) \times t_{\text{rec,max}} \approx \SI{127.5}{\milli\second} \text{ to } \SI{170.0}{\milli\second}
\end{equation}

Because $T_{\text{guard}}$ exceeds the single-frame sampling period ($T_s = \SI{100}{\milli\second}$), the flight software should not rely on transient recovery outrunning the sample clock. Instead, the OBC ADCS task must implement an explicit sample-blanking protocol, automatically discarding the first one to two magnetometer samples ($1\text{--}2 \times T_s$) following any magnetorquer firing event. 

This is a fixed guard window, not a recalibration: Section~\ref{sec:hysteresis} tests separately whether saturation itself leaves a lasting zero-point shift requiring recalibration, independent of this transient-recovery guard-window question. \section{Integrated Board: Field Excitation and Design}
\label{sec:integrated-board}

A hand-held magnet cannot produce a known field, so it cannot measure sensitivity, and it cannot be automated. The integrated board solves both problems: a current-carrying trace produces a field that can be calculated from the measured current, and the microcontroller logs the sensor output without an operator. This board is the low-cost, reusable test method named in the aims, and it is used for every test of sensitivity (H2), lasting change (H4), radiation (H5) and shielding (H6).

\subsection{Integrated Board Design}
\label{sec:integrated-design}

To accurately characterize the sensor's sensitivity without relying on a handheld neodymium magnet, a repeatable and quantifiable test field was required.

A current-carrying PCB trace routed directly beneath the sensor was selected. This DC current induces a magnetic field around the trace that acts upon the TMR sensor to generate a measurable voltage output. It's generated magnetic field is calculated analytically and simulated in Ansys Maxwell, serving as a theoretical benchmark for expected sensor output against practical results. The trace current can be swept up to the laboratory PSU's \SI{6.0}{\ampere} limit. 

While the upper limit of the PSU capability \SI{6.0}{\ampere}  does not generate a strong enough magnetic field to drive the CT100 into saturation, it provides a linear sweep to determine the sensor's sensitivity. Establishing this sensitivity benchmark is critical as later in the characterization, this baseline allows us to measure any resulting hysteresis or degradation in linearity under saturation and/or radiation.

The system architecture of the integrated board revolves around three core components:

\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Microcontroller (MCU):} An STM32G431KBT6 was selected for it's high-performance. The CT100 differential output pair feeds the MCU's internal differential ADC channels directly, sampled at \SI{1.0}{\kilo\hertz}. The MCU features a 12-bit ADC and auto-calibration routines were run to minimise discrepancies between ADC's on MCU on different boards.
    
    \item \textbf{Current Monitor (INA219):} Because the PSU's analog display was not trusted as precise means of determining current, a dedicated INA current-sense with a Kelvin-connected \( \SI{5.0}{\milli\ohm}\) shunt resistor. This was used to back-calculate the magnetic field generated by the trace current acting on the TMR sensor.
    
    \item \textbf{Low-Dropout (LDO) regulator:} The board is powered via a \SI{5.0}{\volt} input rail which is fed into into a LDO regulator to provide a clean \SI{3.30}{\volt} supply to both the sensor and MCU. This LDO also provides a high Power Supply Rejection Ratio (PSRR) and sets the subsequent resolution of the TMR sensor. 
    
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Analytical Field Model}
\label{sec:field-model}

The horizontal magnetic field at the TMR die height (\(d = \SI{0.4229}{\milli\metre}\)) beneath the centre of the trace (\(w = \SI{3.5}{\milli\metre}\)) is modelled using:
\begin{equation}
B_{\text{planar}} = \mu_0 \left[ \frac{I}{\pi w} \arctan\left( \frac{w}{2d} \right) \right].
\label{eq:planar}
\end{equation}

The trace is treated as a thin flat strip of width $w$ carrying the current $I$ uniformly, so it can be split into long straight filaments of width $\mathrm{d}x$, each carrying $\mathrm{d}I = (I/w)\,\mathrm{d}x$. A long straight filament produces a field $\mu_0\,\mathrm{d}I/(2\pi r)$ at distance $r$ \cite{openstax_up2}. At a height $d$ above the centre of the strip, the vertical components from filaments on either side cancel by symmetry, and the horizontal components add, so
\begin{equation}
B = \int_{-w/2}^{w/2} \frac{\mu_0 I}{2\pi w}\,\frac{d}{x^2+d^2}\,\mathrm{d}x = \frac{\mu_0 I}{\pi w}\arctan\!\left(\frac{w}{2d}\right),
\end{equation}
which is Equation~\eqref{eq:planar}.

The distance $d$ in Equation~\eqref{eq:planar} is measured from the mid-plane of the bottom-layer trace to the sensing element. From Table~\ref{tab:stackup}, that mid-plane lies $\SI{0.909}{\milli\metre}$ below the top copper surface: half of the $\SI{0.035}{\milli\metre}$ trace, the $\SI{0.838}{\milli\metre}$ of dielectric, the two $\SI{0.009}{\milli\metre}$ inner copper layers and the $\SI{0.035}{\milli\metre}$ top copper. The sensing element sits a further height $h_s$ above the board inside the package, so
\begin{equation}
d = \SI{0.909}{\milli\metre} + h_s .
\label{eq:d-stackup}
\end{equation}

Normalizing Equation~\eqref{eq:planar} by the trace current $I$ yields the geometric field coupling coefficient $k_B$:
\begin{equation}
k_B = \frac{B_{\text{planar}}}{I} = \frac{\mu_0}{\pi w} \arctan\left( \frac{w}{2d} \right) \approx \SI{0.1108}{\milli\tesla\per\ampere}.
\label{eq:field-coeff}
\end{equation}

Evaluated at a representative excitation current of \(I = \SI{4.175}{\ampere}\), this model yields a theoretical flux density of \SI{462.70}{\micro\tesla}. Combining this field response coefficient $k_B$ with the CT100 sensor's intrinsic sensitivity $S_V$ (\si{\volt\per\milli\tesla}) gives the expected differential voltage change per unit trace current:
\begin{equation}
\frac{\Delta V_{\text{out}}}{\Delta I} = k_B \cdot S_V.
\label{eq:sensitivity-transfer}
\end{equation}

This analytical transfer ratio serves as the theoretical benchmark against which both the Ansys Maxwell finite-element simulations and empirical linear current sweeps are validated.

\subsection{Firmware}
\label{sec:firmware}

The firmware is built upon the STM32 Hardware Abstraction Layer (HAL). The MCU's ADC is configured in differential-pair mode, sampling \(X_1 - X_2\) directly and converting the raw 12-bit value to volts via:
\begin{equation}
X_1 - X_2 = \left( \frac{\text{ADC}_{\text{raw}} - 2048}{2048} \right) \times V_{\text{DD}}
\label{eq:adcconv}
\end{equation}
where 2048 is the mid-scale ADC code corresponding to the zero-differential (balanced-bridge) midpoint. Hardware ADC self-calibration (\texttt{HAL\_ADCEx\_Calibration\_Start}) is executed automatically on every boot before any conversions occur. The MCU continuously polls the INA219 over \(\text{I}^2\text{C}\) for trace current and streams both the current and voltage values out in real time over the Serial Wire Viewer (SWV) debug channel for offline analysis in MATLAB. 


\subsection{Design Note for Future PCB Revisions}
\label{sec:future-pcb-note}

Because the sensor bridge is nominally balanced at \(V_{\text{DD}}/2\) as noted in Section~\ref{sec:bridge-topology}, the ADC channel monitoring it spends its full dynamic range on either side of that midpoint. Any measurement taken near the zero-differential code inherits the ADC's own quantization and calibration uncertainty around that center point.

For intergated boards using the CT100 devicet they should including a hardware offset to bias the bridge's reference point to a non-zero value. This is expected to reduce measurement error introduced by operating close to the ADC's most quantization-sensitive midpoint code.

\subsection{Linear Fitting and the Coefficient of Determination}
\label{sec:fitting}
 
Every current sweep in this report is summarised by a straight line,
\begin{equation}
V_{\text{out}} = m I + c,
\label{eq:linfit}
\end{equation}
where $m$ is the gradient in $\si{\milli\volt\per\ampere}$ and $c$ is the offset, the output at zero trace current. The line is fitted by ordinary least squares, which chooses $m$ and $c$ to minimise the sum of the squared differences between the measured outputs $V_i$ and the fitted values $\hat{V}_i = m I_i + c$.
 
How well the line describes the data is given by the coefficient of determination,
\begin{equation}
R^2 = 1 - \frac{\sum_i (V_i - \hat{V}_i)^2}{\sum_i (V_i - \bar{V})^2},
\label{eq:r2}
\end{equation}
where $\bar{V}$ is the mean of the measured outputs. The numerator is the scatter left over after the fit and the denominator is the total spread of the data, so $R^2$ is the fraction of the variation in the output that the straight line explains. A value of 1 means every point lies exactly on the line; a value of 0 means the line explains none of the variation. In this report $R^2$ is used only as a measure of linearity. A high $R^2$ shows that the output is proportional to current over the sweep, but it does not show that the gradient is correct; that is checked separately against the theoretical gradient. $R^2$ is also unaffected by a constant offset, so a sweep with a large zero-field offset can still have $R^2$ close to 1.
 
The uncertainty of each gradient is given by its standard error,
\begin{equation}
s_m = \sqrt{\frac{\sum_i (V_i - \hat{V}_i)^2 / (n-2)}{\sum_i (I_i - \bar{I})^2}},
\label{eq:slope-se}
\end{equation}
where $n$ is the number of points in the sweep (13 for every sweep here). Gradients are reported as $m \pm s_m$. Two gradients are treated as different only if they differ by clearly more than their combined standard error, $\sqrt{s_{m,1}^2 + s_{m,2}^2}$.

A linear fit of $V_{\text{out}}$ against trace current gives a gradient (the sensitivity to trace current) of
\SI{1.356}{\milli\volt\per\ampere} on the DMM and
\SI{1.315}{\milli\volt\per\ampere} on the MCU.

The CT100 datasheet \cite{ct100_datasheet} specifies a typical full-bridge sensitivity of
\SI{4.5}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}, with a specified range of
\SI{3.8}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla} to
\SI{5.2}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}. At the
\SI{3.32}{\volt} supply voltage used for this characterisation, the
corresponding theoretical voltage sensitivity is therefore

\begin{equation}
S_{V,\mathrm{theory}}
=
S_{\mathrm{CT100}}V_{\mathrm{DD}}
=
\left(\SI{4.5}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}\right)
\left(\SI{3.32}{\volt}\right)
=
\SI{14.94}{\milli\volt\per\milli\tesla}.
\label{eq:ct100-voltage-sensitivity}
\end{equation}

The analytical field model in Equation~\eqref{eq:planar} gives a field
coefficient of approximately
\SI{0.1108}{\milli\tesla\per\ampere} for the trace geometry used in the
test. Combining this field response with the CT100 sensitivity gives the
expected voltage change per unit trace current:

\begin{equation}
\frac{\mathrm{   \Delta}V_{\mathrm{out}}}{\mathrm{   \Delta}I}
=
S_{V,\mathrm{theory}}
\frac{\mathrm{   \Delta}B}{\mathrm{   \Delta}I}
=
\left(\SI{14.94}{\milli\volt\per\milli\tesla}\right)
\left(\SI{0.1108}{\milli\tesla\per\ampere}\right)
=
\SI{1.655}{\milli\volt\per\ampere}.
\label{eq:ct100-voltage-current-slope}
\end{equation}

Thus, for a given trace current, the theoretical sensor output can be
written as

\begin{equation}
V_{\mathrm{out,theory}}
=
V_0
+
\left(\SI{1.655}{\milli\volt\per\ampere}\right)I,
\label{eq:ct100-expected-voltage}
\end{equation}

where $V_0$ is the zero-field output voltage. Figure~\ref{fig:voltage-cross-val} below 
uses a $V_0$ value of \SI{12.980}{\milli\volt} to visually compare the voltage
\emph{slope}; it is not a theoretical prediction of the CT100 offset voltage. Figure~\ref{fig:current-cross-val} plots how close the INA device follows the DMM measurement.

\section{Radiation Testing (Total Ionising Dose)}
\label{sec:radiation-testing}

\subsection{Rationale and Test Isolation Strategy}
\label{sec:rad-rationale}

Crystalline MgO, the MTJ's insulating barrier, is expected to be substantially more radiation-tolerant than the amorphous SiO\textsubscript{2} gate oxide used in conventional silicon devices, for the reasons discussed in Section~\ref{sec:rad-tolerance-lit}. To test this without confounding the result with unrelated failure modes, only the passive CT100 sensor itself was irradiated by itself.


\subsection{Experimental Setup}
\label{sec:rad-setup}

Ten CT100 sensors were exposed using a \SI{320}{\kilo\volt} tube potential (tungsten target, \SI{30}{\degree} anode angle, \SI{3}{\milli\metre} beryllium filter) at a beam current of \SI{10}{\milli\ampere}. The beam was seasoned (ramped up to full parameters) from 15:55 to 16:05 NZST on 22 July 2026, and held at full \SI{320}{\kilo\volt}/\SI{10}{\milli\ampere} output until switch-off at 19:26 NZST, giving \SI{3}{\hour}~\SI{21}{\minute} (\SI{12060}{\second}) of full-power exposure.

The physical test setup inside the irradiation chamber is shown in Figure~\ref{fig:rad_testing_spec}. The dose rate at the sensor location was calculated as \SI{0.0338}{\gray\per\second} (Si), equal to \SI{3.38}{rad(Si)\per\second}, based on the beam path approximated as \SI{315}{\milli\metre} of air followed by \SI{5}{\milli\metre} of aluminium attenuation before reaching the TMR sensor, itself modelled as a silicon block for absorption purposes. Applied over the \SI{12060}{\second} full-power exposure window, this gives a total absorbed dose of \SI{407.6}{\gray} (\SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}(Si)). This dose figure does not include the lower, ramping dose accumulated during the 15:55--16:05 seasoning period, so the total absorbed dose reported here is a conservative estimate.

To contextualize the severity and relevance of this exposure, these parameters were evaluated against the radiation hardness assurance framework outlined by Poivey~\cite{poivey_rha}. Actual natural space environments, such as Low Earth Orbit (LEO) and Geosynchronous Equatorial Orbit (GEO), typically expose components to extremely low, continuous dose rates below \SI{e-3}{rad(Si)\per\second}. The experimental dose rate of \SI{3.38}{rad(Si)\per\second} provides a robust accelerated testing environment that effectively simulates cumulative degradation over a practical laboratory timeframe. Notably, this rate aligns directly with the SCC 22900 (European space) recommendations, which mandate testing dose rates between approximately 0.01 and \SI{10}{rad(Si)\per\second}[cite: 21]. Consequently, the accumulated \SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}(Si) represents a fully compliant and highly relevant benchmark for standard orbital spaceflight qualification.

\section{Magnetic Shielding}
\label{sec:shielding}

\subsection{Enclosure Design}
\label{sec:shielding-enclosure}

The enclosure frame was 3D printed in PLA. To check that the printed frame does not affect the field at the sensor, its relative permeability $\mu_r$ was estimated from published measurements of its magnetic susceptibility $\chi$. The susceptibility describes how strongly a material magnetises in an applied field $H$ where the magnetisation is given by $M = \chi H$. The flux density in the material is then:
\begin{equation}
B = \mu_0 (H + M) = \mu_0 (1 + \chi) H,
\label{eq:susceptibility}
\end{equation}
and comparing this with the definition $B = \mu_0 \mu_r H$ gives:
\begin{equation}
\mu_r = 1 + \chi .
\label{eq:mur-chi}
\end{equation}
Sangal et al. measured the susceptibility of common 3D printing polymers and found all of them to be weakly diamagnetic, with $\chi$ between about $-8.1$ and $-8.7$ parts per million (ppm) for PLA, PETG and ABS \cite{sangal2023}. One part per million is $10^{-6}$, so for PLA, with $\chi \approx -8.3$~ppm,
\begin{equation}
\mu_r = 1 + \chi = 1 + \left(-8.3 \times 10^{-6}\right) \approx 0.9999917 .
\label{eq:mur-pla}
\end{equation}

This differs from the permeability of free space ($\mu_r = 1$) by less than 0.001\%, so the printed frame has no measurable effect on the field at the sensor. It serves only to hold the ferrite plates and the board in place; all of the shielding comes from the ferrite and nanocrystalline layers.

 Surrounding a sensor with a high-permeability material gives magnetic flux a low-reluctance path around the enclosed volume, so an external field is partly diverted away from the sensor. For an ideal closed spherical shell of inner radius $a$, thickness $t \ll a$ and relative permeability $\mu_r \gg 1$, the field inside is reduced by a factor of about $1 + 2\mu_r t/(3a)$ \cite{fitzpatrick_shielding}. A constant ambient field adds only a fixed offset to the output, so shielding is expected to reduce that offset and any noise or drift it causes, not to change the gradient of the response to the trace current (Section~\ref{sec:disc-shielding}).

Mu-metal, a nickel-iron alloy, is the usual choice for DC and low-frequency shielding because of its very high permeability: the initial permeability of fully annealed material is of order 50{,}000 to 100{,}000 depending on the test conditions \cite{msc_mumetal}. It was not used here because of cost, and because shaping the metal after annealing degrades its permeability unless it is re-annealed. \todo{Cite a source for the stress sensitivity of mu-metal, for example a manufacturer handling note.}

Instead, a MnZn ferrite, TDK (EPCOS) SIFERRIT material N95, was selected as a cost-effective alternative. The manufacturer quotes an initial relative permeability $\mu_i$ of 3000 at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ and a saturation flux density $B_S$ of \SI{525}{\milli\tesla} at $25^{\circ}\text{C}$ \cite{tdk_n95}. Six N95 slabs, each \SI{100}{\milli\metre} $\times$ \SI{100}{\milli\metre} $\times$ \SI{5}{\milli\metre}, form the walls of the enclosure. With $\mu_r = 3000$, $t = \SI{5}{\milli\metre}$ and an inner radius of about \SI{50}{\milli\metre}, the closed-shell expression above gives an ideal shielding factor near 200, so a closed shell would cut an external field by about two orders of magnitude. This is an upper bound: the quoted permeability is a small-signal value for a ferrite core rather than a measurement at DC in an enclosure, and the real enclosure has seams and a cable cut-out. The ambient fields to be attenuated (tens of microtesla) are far below the \SI{525}{\milli\tesla} saturation flux density, so the ferrite stays in its linear region.

To enable continuous operation and data logging during testing, power and data connections via an ST-Link programmer must be routed to the enclosed PCB. Consequently, a physical cut-out was designed into the enclosures to accommodate this. However, any gap effects the reluctance of the shielding and allows for ambient magnetic flux to leak and impact the TMR readings.

To reduce this flux leakage, the cut-out was covered with a flexible nanocrystalline shielding foil (YNTECH NF, Qingdao Yunlu Energy Technology). The manufacturer describes its nanocrystalline material as an Fe-based soft magnetic material with a high saturation flux density and a permeability about ten times that of ferrite \cite{yunlu_nanocrystalline}, but gives no specific values for this foil. Measured with a digital caliper, the foil is $\SI{0.11}{\milli\metre}$ thick including its protective plastic film and about $\SI{0.02}{\milli\metre}$ thick with the film removed. The second value is close to the caliper's $\SI{0.01}{\milli\metre}$ resolution, so it is approximate.

Figure \ref{fig:mh-recovery} tracks this transient response, showing that the sensor settles to within 5 mV of its new baseline in 40.1 ms after the negative event, and 48.7 ms after the positive event.

Cumulative Baseline Shift: Figure \ref{fig:mh-baseline} illustrates the permanent offset introduced by each event. The sensor's initial resting baseline of -4.8 mV shifted up slightly to -1.6 mV following the negative saturation. However, the subsequent positive saturation caused a massive upward shift, leaving the final resting baseline at 11.3 mV.

Three observations follow from Table~\ref{tab:baseline-shift}. First, on Board C the negative ceiling moved the baseline by only $+\SI{3.2}{\milli\volt}$ (two ADC steps of $\SI{1.61}{\milli\volt}$), but the positive ceiling that followed moved it by $+\SI{12.9}{\milli\volt}$, leaving the baseline at $+\SI{11.3}{\milli\volt}$, a total shift of $+\SI{16.1}{\milli\volt}$ from the untouched sensor. Second, Board A shows the same pattern, a negligible shift at the negative ceiling and a large one ($+\SI{20.9}{\milli\volt}$) at the positive ceiling. Third, the irradiated Board B shows no comparable shift ($0.0$ and $+\SI{1.6}{\milli\volt}$). Because the negative ceiling was always reached first, these data cannot yet separate the direction of the field from the order of the events, and Board B is a single irradiated unit.

\textbf{Gradient stability.} Despite the shift in offset, the sensor's linear response to current is essentially unaffected. A straight-line fit to each sweep gives a gradient of $1.289 \pm 0.038$~mV/A before saturation and $1.283 \pm 0.030$~mV/A after, a change of only $-0.5\%$, which is far smaller than the standard error of either gradient. The fitted offset at zero current moves from $-4.55$~mV to $+11.50$~mV, a shift of $\SI{16.05}{\milli\volt}$, and $R^2$ stays above 0.99 (0.991 before and 0.994 after). Saturation therefore moves the whole response up without changing its slope or its linearity. This is the pattern seen in panel (b) of Figure~\ref{fig:hysteresis-sweeps}, where the two sweeps overlap once their offsets are removed.

\section{Discussion}
\label{sec:discussion}

This project asked whether a low-cost commercial TMR sensor, the CT100, is operationally viable for CubeSat ADCS/EPS use, focusing on how it behaves when driven into saturation and after irradiation as conducted in Section~\ref{sec:research-question}. The motivation (Section~\ref{sec:intro}) is that CubeSat magnetometers must be small, light and low power, and that TMR offers high sensitivity in a few-millimetre package but has little space heritage (Section~\ref{sec:magnetometer-tech}). This section first checks that the test method can be trusted, then discusses each result against the literature and the CubeSat use case, and ends with the limitations of the work.

\subsection{Validity of the Test Method}
\label{sec:disc-validity}

The results depend on the measurement chain, so it was validated before it was used. The MCU voltage readings agree with the DMM to within $\SI{0.9}{\milli\volt}$, less than one ADC step, and the INA219 current reading agrees with the DMM to within about 1 to 2\% (Section~\ref{sec:instr-validation}). The oscilloscope disagreed with both for DC measurements and was therefore used only for transients, where its time resolution matters. On that basis the MCU alone was used for the shielded sweeps and the hysteresis test, which saved a large amount of test time. The radiation sweeps used both the DMM and the MCU so that they were consistent with the earlier baseline. Two further checks support the method: the analytical field model and the Ansys Maxwell simulation agree within about 8\% (\SI{462.7}{\micro\tesla} and about \SI{500}{\micro\tesla} at \SI{4.175}{\ampere}, Section~\ref{sec:field-validation}), and the measured gradients vary by up to about 7\% between sessions (Section~\ref{sec:session-variance}). The method is therefore good enough for the effects reported here, which are offset shifts of 13 to $\SI{21}{\milli\volt}$ and gradient changes of a few percent. It is not good enough for signals at the level of Earth's field, as discussed in Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations}.

\subsection{Field Model and Sensitivity}
\label{sec:disc-model}

Across the seven current sweeps in this report the measured gradient lies between 1.283 and $\SI{1.447}{\milli\volt\per\ampere}$ (mean about $\SI{1.33}{\milli\volt\per\ampere}$), which is 77 to 87\% of the theoretical $\SI{1.655}{\milli\volt\per\ampere}$ (Figure~\ref{fig:gradient-summary}). Expressed as a sensitivity, the mean gradient corresponds to about $\SI{3.6}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}$, which is about 5\% below the lower end of the datasheet range of 3.8 to $\SI{5.2}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}$ \cite{ct100_datasheet}. Figure~\ref{fig:sensitivity-comparison} compares the four test conditions against the theoretical line with each sweep's offset removed, so that only the gradients are compared. All measured lines are close together and below the theoretical line.

This shortfall is small enough to be explained by two things that were not controlled. The first is the field model: the coupling constant $k_B$ depends strongly on the distance $d$ from the trace to the sensing element, which is not known precisely inside the package (Section~\ref{sec:field-model}). The second is part-to-part tolerance, since the datasheet allows sensitivities down to $\SI{3.8}{\milli\volt\per\volt\per\milli\tesla}$. The ambient field is not a candidate explanation: a constant ambient field adds an offset to the output but does not change its gradient. The measured response is linear (R$^2$ of about 0.97 or higher in the sweeps fitted in this report) and the transfer from trace current to output is therefore well described by a single gradient. For a CubeSat designer the practical conclusion is that the datasheet sensitivity can be used as a starting point, but that each unit should be calibrated, as with any magnetometer. \subsection{Saturation Recovery and What It Means for a CubeSat}
\label{sec:disc-recovery}

Table~\ref{tab:recovery-summary} summarises the saturation recovery across all samples. The ceiling is within a few percent of $V_{\text{DD}}/3$ on every unit, confirming the bridge model of Section~\ref{sec:bridge-topology}, and it is symmetric about zero. Recovery takes 40 to $\SI{85}{\milli\second}$ depending on the unit, and on the three integrated boards the decay is a clean exponential with a time constant of 9 to $\SI{14}{\milli\second}$, with no difference between the non-irradiated and irradiated boards.

What this means depends on how often the on-board computer reads the magnetometer. Published CubeSat designs read magnetometers at rates from about \SI{1}{\hertz}, the rate of the whole attitude-control loop on InnoCube \cite{djebko_lelar}, up to about \SI{10}{\hertz} for a commercial CubeSat magnetometer \cite{nss_magnetometer}. At \SI{10}{\hertz} the sample interval is $T_s = \SI{100}{\milli\second}$, so the worst measured recovery time of $\SI{85}{\milli\second}$ (Sample 4) leaves a margin of only $\SI{15}{\milli\second}$. Comparing the recovery \emph{frequency} ($1/t_{\text{rec}} \approx 12$ to $\SI{25}{\hertz}$) with the sampling frequency is misleading, because a saturating event is not synchronised with the sampling clock. In the worst case the event ends just before a scheduled sample, and that sample is taken while the sensor is still recovering. An unrecovered reading would appear to a state estimator as a sudden impulse in the field and could trigger a false correction. The design quantity is therefore a guard window that covers the worst-case recovery with margin. Applying a conventional safety factor of 1.5 to 2 to the worst measured recovery gives a guard window of $\SI{127.5}{}$ to $\SI{170}{\milli\second}$:
\begin{equation}
T_{\text{guard}} = (1.5 \text{ to } 2) \times t_{\text{rec,max}} = (1.5 \text{ to } 2) \times \SI{85}{\milli\second} \approx \SI{127.5}{} \text{ to } \SI{170}{\milli\second}.
\end{equation}
This exceeds one sample interval at \SI{10}{\hertz}, so the first one or two readings after a saturating event should be discarded. At a \SI{1}{\hertz} loop the recovery is over long before the next sample and no blanking is needed. At sample rates of hundreds of hertz, such as the \SI{500}{\hertz} maximum configurable rate of one commercial magnetometer \cite{mm200_datasheet}, tens of consecutive samples would be affected, so a longer blanking period, or fusion with another attitude sensor during recovery, would be required.

Saturation is easy to detect in flight, because the output goes to a ceiling of about $\pm V_{\text{DD}}/3$ ($\pm\SI{1.1}{\volt}$), which is more than a thousand times larger than the output for Earth's field. Flight software can therefore recognise a saturating event from the reading itself and then apply the guard window. The sources of such events are off-nominal (Section~\ref{sec:strong-field-lit}): a normally operating magnetorquer of about $\SI{0.2}{\ampere\metre\squared}$ gives a few millitesla at a few centimetres \cite{isis_imtq}, which would shift the output by tens of millivolts but is well inside the CT100's $\pm\SI{50}{\milli\tesla}$ range. Saturation would instead come from a much closer or stronger source, such as a magnet near the sensor or magnetising equipment during ground handling. Stress-testing saturation is still worthwhile because it characterises the worst case the sensor must survive and recover from, regardless of which source causes it.

The measured recovery times should be treated with caution because the magnet was withdrawn by hand. The datasheet bandwidth of $\SI{1}{\kilo\hertz}$ \cite{digikey_ct100} implies an electrical response time of well under a millisecond, so the $\SI{9}{}$ to $\SI{14}{\milli\second}$ time constants are not set by the sensor's bandwidth. They come either from the speed at which the field was removed or from the magnetic relaxation of the sensor, and the present measurement cannot separate the two (Section~\ref{sec:disc-limitations}).

\subsection{Offset Shift After Saturation}
\label{sec:disc-hysteresis}

The most significant finding of this work is that saturation moves the zero-field output without changing the gradient. On a fresh sensor with no magnetic history, two saturation events shifted the baseline by $\SI{16.1}{\milli\volt}$ (from $-4.8$ to $+\SI{11.3}{\milli\volt}$), while the gradient changed by only $-0.5\%$ and the response stayed linear (Section~\ref{sec:hysteresis}). Board A showed a similar shift of $+\SI{20.9}{\milli\volt}$ at the positive ceiling. At the sensor's sensitivity of about $\SI{14.9}{\milli\volt\per\milli\tesla}$ a shift of 16 to $\SI{21}{\milli\volt}$ is equivalent to a field of 1.1 to $\SI{1.4}{\milli\tesla}$, which is at least 15 times Earth's field of 25 to $\SI{65}{\micro\tesla}$ \cite{wmm}. This is consistent with the manufacturer's statement that a strong field can leave a stable, irreversible offset shift and that recalibration is recommended \cite{allegro_an133}, and it puts a size on the effect for this part. The datasheet offset specification of $\pm 5$~mV/V ($\pm\SI{16.6}{\milli\volt}$ at $V_{\text{DD}} = \SI{3.32}{\volt}$) \cite{ct100_datasheet} would still be met after the shift, so the part would not be flagged as out of specification even though the shift is large compared with the geomagnetic signal.

Because the gradient and linearity are unchanged, the shift behaves like a movement of the operating point, as expected if the free layer relaxes to a slightly different resting state after saturation. For a CubeSat this means that a one-point re-zeroing of the offset is sufficient after a saturating event, and that a full recalibration is not needed. The saturation can be detected from the reading itself (Section~\ref{sec:disc-recovery}), so the recommended procedure is to detect the ceiling, discard the guard window, and then re-measure the zero-field output before trusting the magnetometer again.

This also explains the difference in baseline between the non-irradiated and irradiated sweeps (about $+\SI{12}{\milli\volt}$ against about $+\SI{1}{\milli\volt}$). The non-irradiated board had been saturated before its sweeps, the irradiated board had not, and the fresh sensor shows that saturation alone moves the baseline by this amount. It is therefore not evidence of a radiation effect on the offset. One point remains open: Board B's own saturation events moved its baseline by only $\SI{1.6}{\milli\volt}$, so it is not yet known whether irradiated sensors respond to saturation differently. The evidence is also limited by the order of the events, because in every run the negative ceiling was reached before the positive one, so the direction of the field and the order of the events cannot yet be separated.

\subsection{Radiation Tolerance}
\label{sec:disc-radiation}

Ten sensors were exposed to $\SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}$(Si), and three were tested (two on sensor-only boards and one on the integrated board). No change was found in the saturation ceiling (0.5\% difference), the recovery time (1.7\%), the recovery time constant (13.3 against $\SI{13.2}{\milli\second}$) or the gradient (93.6 to 99.8\% of the non-irradiated value, within session-to-session variation). This is what the literature predicts: MTJ sensors have been shown to tolerate $\SI{5}{\mega rad}$(Si) \cite{tmr-bias-radiation} because the crystalline MgO barrier has far fewer trap sites for radiation-induced charge than the amorphous oxides in CMOS devices \cite{tid-basics-lanl}. The dose used here is modest by comparison with that work and with the $\SI{300}{\kilo rad}$(Si) used for a COTS magnetometer \cite{regoli2020}, but it is above the $\SI{10}{\kilo rad}$ component-level dose quoted for a commercial CubeSat magnetometer \cite{nss_magnetometer}. The contribution is device-level data for one low-cost packaged part, including, to the author's knowledge, saturation recovery after irradiation. The test has several limits. The sensors were not monitored during exposure, so transient effects that vanish afterwards were not seen, and the bias condition during exposure needs to be stated (Section~\ref{sec:rad-setup}). X-rays test ionising dose only, not displacement damage or single-event effects. The comparison is between different units with different magnetic histories, with two sensor-only samples and one integrated board per group. The result should therefore be read as a first indication that permanent radiation damage at this dose is small, not as a qualification.

\subsection{Magnetic Shielding}
\label{sec:disc-shielding}

Shielding did not measurably change the response. The gradient changed by $+10\%$ on the non-irradiated board and $+2.3\%$ on the irradiated board (Figure~\ref{fig:shielding-overlay}), and a repeat shielded sweep differed from the first by 7\% (Section~\ref{sec:session-variance}), so the shielding effect is smaller than the session-to-session variation. This should not be read as proof that the shield does not work, for three reasons. First, a constant ambient field changes the offset and not the gradient, so a gradient comparison is an indirect test of shielding at best. Second, Earth's field of tens of microtesla is only about $\SI{0.7}{\milli\volt}$ at the CT100's sensitivity, less than one ADC step of $\SI{1.61}{\milli\volt}$, so even a perfect shield would not have produced a visible change with this measurement chain. Third, the enclosure is far from ideal: a closed shell of the N95 ferrite would at best reduce an external field by a factor of about 200 (Section~\ref{sec:shielding-enclosure}), and the real enclosure has seams and a cable cut-out. The ferrite close to the trace may also change the field of the trace itself, which could account for the $+10\%$ gradient change on one board. A proper test would apply a known external field with and without the enclosure and compare the zero-current baseline and noise on a higher-resolution measurement. Mu-metal could then be tried if the ferrite proves insufficient. For flight, magnetometer placement away from sources matters more than shielding the sensor \cite{nss_magnetometer}.

\subsection{Power Consumption}
\label{sec:disc-power}

Power is a first-order constraint on a CubeSat, so the cost of the sensing element is relevant. The bridge draws current $V_{\text{DD}}/R$, where $R$ is the element resistance, because two elements in series form each of two parallel branches. With the datasheet element resistance of 20 to $\SI{40}{\kilo\ohm}$ \cite{ct100_datasheet}, the bridge draws about 82 to $\SI{165}{\micro\ampere}$, or 0.27 to $\SI{0.54}{\milli\watt}$, at $V_{\text{DD}} = \SI{3.30}{\volt}$, and this falls with the square of the supply voltage down to the datasheet minimum of $\SI{1.0}{\volt}$. For comparison, the AMR-based MAGIC instrument draws $\SI{0.5}{\watt}$ \cite{magic-radcube} and a commercial CubeSat magnetometer about $\SI{0.7}{\watt}$ \cite{nss_magnetometer}. These are complete instruments with electronics, so the comparison is not like for like, but it shows that the sensing element itself is a negligible part of a magnetometer's power budget. \subsection{Limitations and Better Ways to Measure the Response}
\label{sec:disc-limitations}

The main weakness of the current method is the saturating stimulus. A hand-held magnet gives an uncontrolled withdrawal speed, an unknown field at the sensor, and poor repeatability, so recovery times include the operator and cannot be compared closely between units. A better method would use an electromagnet driven through a fast switch, so that the field is removed in a controlled, repeatable way, with the coil current and the sensor output recorded together on the oscilloscope and several repeats taken to give a mean and standard deviation. A linear actuator moving a magnet at a fixed speed would also work. The trace used for the current sweeps cannot saturate the sensor, because a field of about $\SI{20}{\milli\tesla}$ would need on the order of $\SI{180}{\ampere}$.

Other limitations are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Resolution.} With an unamplified 12-bit ADC, one step of $\SI{1.61}{\milli\volt}$ is about $\SI{108}{\micro\tesla}$ at the CT100's sensitivity, larger than Earth's field, and the noise in the SWV traces analysed (a few tenths of a millivolt) is equivalent to roughly 14 to $\SI{40}{\micro\tesla}$. This work therefore shows that the CT100 survives and recovers from strong fields and radiation, but not that it can resolve the geomagnetic field. That would need a gain stage, oversampling or a higher-resolution ADC, which would in turn affect the recovery measurements through amplifier slew rate.
\item \textbf{Oscilloscope resolution.} At $\SI{500}{\milli\volt}$/div the oscilloscope steps are about $\SI{5}{\milli\volt}$, so the sensor-only baselines are not interpretable and the ceilings carry about $\pm\SI{5}{\milli\volt}$ of uncertainty.
\item \textbf{Sample size and design.} There are two sensor-only samples per group, one board per group on the integrated board, a single dose level, and the irradiated and non-irradiated comparison is across different units.
\item \textbf{Hysteresis test.} The two sensors tested were saturated negative first, and only one fresh sensor was tested in a controlled before-and-after sequence.
\item \textbf{Field model.} The trace-to-die distance used in the field model needs to be confirmed (Section~\ref{sec:field-model}).
\item \textbf{Scope of the project.} Temperature effects were studied separately by the project partner and are not covered here.
\end{itemize}




\section{Conclusions and Future Work}
\label{sec:conclusions}

\textbf{Conclusions.} This project characterised a low-cost commercial TMR sensor, the CT100, using two purpose-built PCBs, a field model checked against a finite-element simulation, and instruments that were validated against each other. Within the limits above, the findings are as follows.
\begin{itemize}
\item The saturation ceiling is within about 5\% of $\pm V_{\text{DD}}/3$, as derived from the datasheet resistance range, on every unit tested, and it is symmetric about zero.
\item Recovery from saturation takes 40 to $\SI{85}{\milli\second}$, as an exponential decay with a time constant of about 9 to $\SI{14}{\milli\second}$. At a $\SI{10}{\hertz}$ magnetometer rate this requires the first one to two samples after a saturating event to be discarded, while at \SI{1}{\hertz} no action is needed.
\item Saturation shifts the zero-field offset by about 13 to $\SI{21}{\milli\volt}$ (1 to 1.4 mT equivalent) without changing the gradient or the linearity, so a one-point re-zeroing after a saturating event is required. This matches the manufacturer's note on offset shift and puts a size on it for the CT100.
\item The measured gradient is 77 to 87\% of the theoretical value, consistent with the datasheet tolerance and the uncertainty of the field model. The current-to-output response is linear with R$^2$ above 0.97.
\item No change attributable to radiation was found in ceiling, recovery, time constant or gradient after $\SI{40.76}{\kilo rad}$(Si), as expected from the literature on MTJ sensors.
\item Ferrite shielding did not measurably change the response. The test was insensitive to what shielding does, so shielding effectiveness remains unresolved.
\item The sensing element draws an estimated 0.27 to $\SI{0.54}{\milli\watt}$, a negligible part of a magnetometer power budget.
\end{itemize}

\textbf{Significance.} Taken together, these results suggest that the CT100 is a promising low-cost COTS candidate for CubeSat magnetometry: it tolerates ionising radiation at the dose tested, survives saturation, recovers within tens of milliseconds, and only shifts its zero point, which can be corrected by re-zeroing. They also provide a reusable, low-cost test method for other COTS TMR parts. The main reservations are that the ability to resolve the geomagnetic field was not demonstrated, the dose was modest, and the sample sizes were small, so more testing is needed before flight use.

\textbf{Future work.}
\begin{itemize}
\item Replace the hand-held magnet with a controlled, repeatable saturation source (an electromagnet with a fast switch) and record several repeats per sample.
\item Repeat the hysteresis test on several fresh sensors, with the positive ceiling first as well as the negative, and on a fresh irradiated sensor, and test a re-zeroing procedure in firmware that detects the ceiling, applies the guard window and re-measures the offset.
\item Monitor sensors during irradiation, state the bias condition, and extend the tests to more dose levels and to proton irradiation.
\item Test shielding properly with a known external field and a higher-resolution measurement, and try mu-metal.
\item Add a gain stage or a higher-resolution ADC, characterise the noise floor in field units, and measure the supply current.
\item Confirm the trace-to-die distance in the field model, and combine these results with the thermal-cycling results of the project partner.
\end{itemize}
